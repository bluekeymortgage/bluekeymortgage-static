Reverse Mortgage vs HELOC in Ontario: An Honest Comparison

By Ragini Domenichini, Mortgage Agent Level 2 (FSRA licence M22004226), BRX Mortgage Inc. (FSRA #13463) · Published July 10, 2026 · Updated November 16, 2026 · 8 minute read

Quick answer: A HELOC usually has a lower interest rate, but you need income to qualify and you make regular payments. A reverse mortgage needs no income test and no required monthly payment, but the rate is usually higher and the balance grows over time. Which one fits comes down to your cash flow, your plans, and whether a monthly payment feels comfortable.

Homeowners ask me almost every week which is better, a HELOC or a reverse mortgage. The good news is that both can be the right tool. The honest answer depends on whether you can qualify for and comfortably carry the payments on one of them, and I will walk you through exactly how to tell.

The short answer, up front

I don't like burying the lead, so here it is: if you can comfortably qualify for a home equity line of credit (HELOC) and can afford the payments, that is often the lower cost way to borrow against your home. A reverse mortgage shines in a different situation, when you either can't qualify for a HELOC (or a further advance on your existing mortgage), or you can qualify but would rather not carry a required monthly payment on a fixed retirement income.

Neither product is a trick and neither is the only smart choice. They solve different problems, and both can be a genuinely good fit. Let's go through both calmly, including where each one has tradeoffs.

How a HELOC works, in Ontario terms

A home equity line of credit is revolving credit secured against your home. You are approved for a credit limit and you can borrow, repay, and borrow again as you need. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada notes you may borrow up to 65% of the value of your home, and that you only pay interest on the amount you actually borrow.

To get one, you go through a fairly conventional lending process. FCAC explains that you need enough equity (generally more than 35% for a standalone HELOC, or 20% for a HELOC combined with a mortgage), and you must pass a lender stress test that proves you can afford payments at a qualifying rate. You make regular payments on whatever balance you carry, either interest only or part principal plus interest, and you can pay the balance at any time.

What makes a HELOC attractive

  • Usually a lower interest rate. FCAC states that a reverse mortgage rate is usually higher than a HELOC or a regular mortgage, so when you qualify and can make the payments, a HELOC often costs less over time.
  • You control the balance. Pay it down whenever you like, borrow again later, and interest only accrues on what you have drawn.
  • Flexibility for planned needs. Helpful for bridging a purchase, funding a renovation you will repay from other savings, or covering a short gap.

Where a HELOC can be harder for retirees

  • You have to qualify. If your retirement income is modest, fixed, or comes mostly from investments that don't show up cleanly on a lender's statement, passing the stress test can be genuinely difficult. This is the single most common reason people look at a reverse mortgage instead.
  • Payments are required. FCAC cautions that if rates rise your payments can get harder, that easy access to credit can tempt more debt, and that if you only pay the interest you will not pay off the loan.
  • It can be reduced or called. A HELOC is usually a demand product, so the lender can lower your available limit or, in some circumstances, ask for repayment. FCAC also notes that if you don't pay, the lender may take possession of your home, and if you sell you must repay the HELOC.

How a reverse mortgage works, in Ontario terms

A reverse mortgage is also a loan secured against your home, available to homeowners 55 and older, but it flips the qualification model. FCAC notes there is no required payment until the loan is due (when you move out, sell, the last borrower passes away, or you default), and that you may usually borrow up to 55% of your home's current value. You can take the money as a lump sum, as scheduled advances, or a mix, and you remain the owner. I cover the full mechanics, including realistic amounts by age, on the main reverse mortgage page.

Because payments are not required, interest is added to the balance, so what you owe grows over time unless you choose to pay some interest voluntarily, which most lenders allow. The tradeoff for that freedom from a monthly payment is a rate that FCAC says is usually higher than a HELOC or a regular mortgage. Good to know: FCAC confirms that money borrowed through a reverse mortgage does not affect your Old Age Security or Guaranteed Income Supplement, although income earned by investing the funds may be treated differently.

What makes a reverse mortgage attractive

  • No income or credit test in the conventional sense. Approval is based mainly on your age, the property, and your equity, a real advantage if your retirement income doesn't fit a lender's spreadsheet.
  • No required monthly payment. Nothing comes due while you live in the home, keep taxes and insurance current, and maintain the property.
  • It can't be called for financial reasons. Meet your basic obligations and the loan stays in place regardless of what happens to your income or the wider market.
  • It does not reduce OAS or GIS. Per FCAC, the money you borrow does not affect those benefits.

Where a reverse mortgage costs more

  • Higher interest rate than a HELOC. Because the rate is usually higher and no payments are required, interest adds up more quickly over a decade or more.
  • The balance grows if you don't pay interest. Each year interest is added to what you owe, which reduces the equity left for you or your estate.
  • Setup costs. FCAC lists possible costs such as an appraisal, a setup fee, legal fees, closing costs, and prepayment charges. In Ontario, independent legal advice is required before you complete a reverse mortgage. These costs can often come out of the proceeds rather than your pocket.
  • It may need to replace a current mortgage or HELOC. FCAC notes a reverse mortgage may limit other financing secured by your home, so you may need to pay off and close a current mortgage or HELOC. The lender may let you use the reverse mortgage funds to do that.

The rule of thumb I actually use with clients

When someone asks me which one to choose, I walk through this in order:

  1. Can you qualify for a HELOC on your current income? If a lender would approve you comfortably and you're confident you can manage the payments for years, not just this year, that is often the lower cost tool.
  2. Would the payments stretch your budget? A HELOC that technically qualifies you but leaves your monthly budget uncomfortably tight trades one kind of stress for another.
  3. Is your income mostly fixed, modest, or hard to document? If a HELOC application would be a stretch or a decline, a reverse mortgage may be the realistic option, not a last resort.
  4. How long do you plan to stay in the home? Setup costs on either product are easier to justify over a longer horizon. If you expect to move within a couple of years, both options deserve a careful look against simply downsizing.

My honest take, after years of this work: if you qualify comfortably for a HELOC and can service it without strain, that is often the better value. If that door isn't realistically open, or the idea of a required payment on a fixed income weighs on you, a reverse mortgage is a legitimate, regulated tool built for exactly that situation, and nothing to feel embarrassed about needing. A blended approach: use both, in sequence

One pattern I see often with Ontario retirees: start with a HELOC while your income comfortably covers the payments, then treat a reverse mortgage as the next step if your circumstances change, a spouse passes away and household income drops, health costs rise, or you simply decide you'd rather not carry a payment anymore. The reverse mortgage proceeds can pay off the HELOC balance and replace it with a structure that needs no monthly payment. It isn't right for everyone, and the numbers only make sense with an honest look at your specific equity and age at the time, but it helps to know the door isn't closed once you've chosen a path. What neither product should feel like

Whichever way you lean, a few things should always be true. You should never feel rushed into signing anything. You should get a plain English explanation of exactly what the money costs you, not just what it gives you. And with a reverse mortgage specifically, Ontario law requires independent legal advice from your own lawyer before you complete one. That is not a formality to skip past, it is your safeguard. Questions people ask about this

Which usually has a lower interest rate, a HELOC or a reverse mortgage?

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada says a reverse mortgage interest rate is usually higher than a HELOC or a regular mortgage. So if you qualify for a HELOC and can comfortably make the payments, it often costs less over time. A reverse mortgage costs more in interest specifically because no monthly payments are required and the interest is added to the balance. Which fits is not universal; it depends on your income, your timeline, and your goals.

Do I need good credit and income to qualify for either one?

For a HELOC, yes. FCAC notes you need enough equity and must pass a lender stress test that proves you can afford the payments at a qualifying rate. For a reverse mortgage there is no income or credit test in the same sense; approval is based mainly on your age, the property, and your equity. This is one of the clearest dividing lines between the two.

How much can I borrow with each one?

FCAC says you may be able to borrow up to 65% of your home's value with a HELOC, and usually up to 55% of the appraised value with a reverse mortgage, minus the balance of any existing mortgage. Your actual limit depends on the lender, the property and, for a reverse mortgage, the age of the youngest owner. Amounts are set by the lender and are not guaranteed.

Can a bank reduce or call in a HELOC?

Yes. A HELOC is usually a demand product, so the lender can reduce your available limit or, in some circumstances, call the balance due, particularly if your situation changes or property values drop. A reverse mortgage cannot be called for financial reasons as long as you keep the home as your principal residence, pay your property taxes and insurance, and maintain the property.

Can I switch from a HELOC to a reverse mortgage later?

Often, yes. A common path is to use a HELOC while you can comfortably service the payments, then move to a reverse mortgage later if income drops or the payments become a strain. The reverse mortgage proceeds can be used to pay off the HELOC balance. Whether it makes sense depends on your equity and age at the time, so it is worth reviewing with a licensed agent before you need to make the switch.

Can I have a HELOC and a reverse mortgage at the same time?

Usually not. FCAC notes a reverse mortgage may limit other financing secured by your home, and an existing mortgage or HELOC may need to be paid off and closed. The lender may let you use the reverse mortgage funds to pay those off.
