Reverse mortgage basics

Can You Get a Reverse Mortgage If You Still Have a Mortgage in Ontario?

By Ragini Domenichini, Mortgage Agent Level 2 · October 1, 2026 · 8 minute read

Usually, yes. Your current mortgage does not stay in place. It is normally paid in full from the reverse mortgage proceeds, and only the amount left after that payout and the closing costs is available to you.

The short answer

You do not need to own your Ontario home free and clear before asking about a reverse mortgage. Many homeowners use part of the new loan to pay off a regular mortgage that is still outstanding.

The important detail is that the two mortgages do not normally continue side by side. When the reverse mortgage closes, your lawyer uses its proceeds to repay the existing mortgage and any other secured debt the new lender requires to be cleared. The reverse mortgage is then registered against the home.

This means the useful question is not simply, “How much could I be approved for?” It is, “How much would be left after everything that must be paid?”

What happens to the mortgage you already have?

The reverse mortgage lender will review the debts registered against the property. These can include a first mortgage, a second mortgage, or a home equity line of credit. The lender will normally require those debts to be paid and discharged as part of the closing.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada explains that a reverse mortgage lender may require loans or lines of credit secured by the home to be paid and closed. Current information from major Canadian reverse mortgage lenders also says an existing mortgage can be paid from the reverse mortgage proceeds.

Your lawyer handles the money on closing. The lender does not hand you the full approved amount and ask you to pay the old mortgage later. Required payouts are made first. You receive only the net amount that remains.

The number that matters is the net amount

A reverse mortgage approval is a gross amount. Before you decide whether it solves the problem, subtract every amount that must be paid at closing:

  • The current mortgage payout
  • Any secured line of credit or second mortgage that must be cleared
  • A possible prepayment charge for ending the current mortgage
  • Mortgage discharge and administration charges
  • The reverse mortgage setup, appraisal, legal, and closing costs

The result is the approximate cash available for your goals. If the purpose is simply to remove a monthly payment, there may be little or no extra cash needed. If you also need money for home repairs, care, or income support, the remaining amount matters much more.

A simple example

Suppose a homeowner is approved for a $350,000 reverse mortgage. The current mortgage payout is $205,000 and the secured line of credit is $30,000. Assume another $6,000 for the current mortgage payout charge and the legal, appraisal, setup, discharge, and closing costs.

  • $350,000 gross reverse mortgage amount
  • Minus $205,000 current mortgage payout
  • Minus $30,000 secured line of credit
  • Minus $6,000 estimated costs and charges
  • Equals about $109,000 remaining

This is an illustration, not a quote or a promise of approval. The actual amount and costs depend on the borrowers, the property, both mortgage contracts, the lender, and the closing date. Ask for the figures in writing before relying on them.

Do the payout math first. A large approval can sound reassuring, but the net amount is what tells you whether the plan actually meets your needs.

What if the approval is not enough?

Imagine the reverse mortgage approval is $240,000, but the current mortgage payout is $250,000 before any closing costs. There is a shortfall. The new lender will not normally leave part of the old mortgage unpaid and take its place behind that loan.

The reverse mortgage may be unable to close unless the homeowner can cover the shortfall and the costs from another acceptable source. That is why an early estimate needs to include an up to date payout statement, not just the balance shown on an online banking screen.

If the numbers do not work, that does not mean there are no options. It means the next step should be a fair comparison of renewing or refinancing the existing mortgage, selling or downsizing, reducing the amount needed, or waiting until the mortgage reaches a better payout date.

What happens to the monthly mortgage payment?

Once the regular mortgage is paid out, its monthly payment ends. A reverse mortgage normally does not require regular principal and interest payments while you continue to meet the contract conditions.

That can create breathing room in a retirement budget, but the debt has not disappeared. Interest is added to the reverse mortgage balance, so the amount owing grows over time and the equity left in the home is reduced.

You also remain responsible for the costs of owning the home. These usually include property taxes, home insurance, condominium fees where applicable, and reasonable maintenance. Missing those obligations can place the mortgage in default.

Check the cost of ending your current mortgage

The balance shown on your statement may not be the full payout. If you have a closed mortgage, paying it off before the end of the term can create a prepayment charge. The method and amount depend on the contract. There may also be an administration or discharge fee.

The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada guide to breaking a mortgage contract recommends checking the costs before ending a mortgage early. Ask your current lender for:

  • A written payout statement for the expected closing date
  • The prepayment charge shown separately
  • Any administration and discharge charges
  • The date when the charge could change
  • Whether your mortgage is open or closed

If renewal is close, compare the cost of acting now with the cost of waiting. Timing should not be the only consideration, especially if cash flow is urgent, but it can materially change the math.

Is replacing the regular mortgage always the right move?

No. Removing a required monthly payment can be useful, but it is not free. Reverse mortgage interest rates are generally higher than rates for a regular mortgage or secured line of credit, and the interest compounds when it is not paid.

A reverse mortgage may deserve consideration when the existing payment is straining the household budget, the homeowner wants to remain in the property, and the numbers leave enough room for the intended goals. It may be less attractive when:

  • The current mortgage payment is comfortably affordable
  • The current interest rate is low and the homeowner expects to sell soon
  • The reverse mortgage amount barely covers the payout and costs
  • Preserving as much home equity as possible is the highest priority
  • A lower cost option is available and manageable

It is worth reading the full comparison of a reverse mortgage and a home equity line of credit. The cheaper product is not always the safer product for a household on a fixed income, because a line of credit has qualification requirements and monthly interest payments.

Alternatives worth comparing

A fair review should include more than one route. Depending on income, credit, equity, and goals, the alternatives may include:

  • Renewing or refinancing the current mortgage. This may keep the rate lower, but regular payments and qualification still apply.
  • Extending the amortization if available. A longer repayment period may lower the monthly payment, while increasing the total interest cost.
  • Using a secured line of credit. It may cost less, but payments are required and rates can change.
  • Borrowing a smaller amount. Taking only what is needed can reduce future interest and protect more equity.
  • Selling or downsizing. This can remove debt without replacing it, but moving costs and the price of the next home must be counted.

If the real goal is to clear several monthly debts, the article on paying off debt in retirement explains why lowering the monthly outflow and lowering the total borrowing cost are not always the same thing.

What to gather before asking for numbers

A useful first conversation is easier when the facts are on the table. Gather:

  • The most recent mortgage statement
  • A current payout quote if the lender will provide one
  • Statements for any secured line of credit or second mortgage
  • The names and ages of everyone registered on title
  • The property address, type, and an honest estimate of value
  • Property tax, insurance, and condominium fee information
  • The amount you need after the old debts and costs are paid
  • How long you expect to remain in the home

The age of the registered owners, the property's value, type and location, and the lender's current rules all affect the amount that may be available. My guide to how much you may be able to borrow explains those factors without promising a number.

Seven questions to ask before proceeding

  1. What is the gross reverse mortgage amount, and what is the estimated net amount to me?
  2. Which existing debts must be paid and closed?
  3. What is the exact current mortgage payout for the expected closing date?
  4. Which setup, appraisal, legal, discharge, and administration costs apply?
  5. Is there a prepayment charge on my current mortgage, and when could it change?
  6. How will the reverse mortgage balance grow if I make no voluntary payments?
  7. What lower cost or lower debt alternatives should I compare?

Questions people ask about this

Can I get a reverse mortgage if I still have a mortgage?

Usually, yes. The existing mortgage is normally paid in full from the reverse mortgage proceeds. Any money left after the payout, secured debts, and closing costs is available to you.

Does my old mortgage stay in place?

Usually not. The reverse mortgage lender normally requires the existing mortgage and other loans secured by the home to be paid and discharged when the new reverse mortgage closes.

How much equity do I need for a reverse mortgage?

There is no single answer for every homeowner. The amount available depends on factors such as the age of the registered owners, the home's value, property type and location, current secured debts, and the lender's rules. The approved amount must also be enough to clear required payouts and costs.

What if the reverse mortgage approval is lower than my current mortgage payout?

The reverse mortgage may not be able to close unless you can cover the shortfall and all required costs from another acceptable source. Ask for a written payout statement and a detailed estimate before making plans.

Will my monthly mortgage payment disappear?

The payment on the old mortgage ends after it is paid out. A reverse mortgage normally has no required regular principal and interest payments, but interest is added to the balance. You must still pay property taxes, maintain home insurance, and keep the home in reasonable condition.
