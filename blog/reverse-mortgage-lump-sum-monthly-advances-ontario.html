Borrowing at a pace that fits your life

Reverse Mortgage: Lump Sum or Monthly Advances in Ontario?

By Ragini Domenichini, Mortgage Agent Level 2 · October 12, 2026 · 8 minute read

Do you need money for one expense, a little help each month, or a mix of both? With a reverse mortgage, when you receive the money matters as much as how much you qualify for. Here is how to compare the choices without confusing borrowed cash with retirement income.

The short answer: there is more than one way to receive the money

Canadian reverse mortgage products may offer a lump sum, an initial amount followed by later advances, or a schedule of payments to you. Some allow a combination. Not every lender or product offers every option.

The best starting question is not “How much can I get?” It is “What do I need the money to do, and when?” A roof replacement, a mortgage payout, and a monthly household budget shortfall are different needs. They deserve different borrowing plans.

An important distinction: an advance is money the lender releases to you. It is not a repayment you make to the lender. Monthly reverse mortgage advances are still debt secured against your home, not a pension, government benefit, or promise of income for life.

Option 1: a lump sum for a known expense

A lump sum may suit a cost you can identify now, such as repaying an existing mortgage or paying for a specific home improvement. You receive an agreed amount at the start, rather than requesting smaller amounts over time.

The caution is timing. Once money has been advanced, it starts contributing to the cost of your loan even if it is sitting untouched in a bank account. FCAC warns that taking the whole amount when you do not need it right away can be an expensive way to borrow.

Before deciding, separate the amount approved from the cash available to spend. Existing debts secured against your home may need to be paid first. Fees can also reduce the proceeds or be added to the balance. The guide to getting a reverse mortgage with an existing mortgage explains that distinction.

Ask whether the proposed product is designed for a single advance. Do not assume that a low rate quote also includes the ability to borrow more later.

Option 2: monthly or quarterly advances for a recurring gap

If your pension covers most expenses but not all of them, scheduled advances may be worth comparing. A regular amount can make budgeting more predictable than taking a large amount and deciding how much to spend each month.

For example, HomeEquity Bank describes its Income Advantage product as offering monthly or quarterly advances alongside an initial lump sum. EQ Bank also describes recurring advances, but its published advance rules restrict that option to an adjustable interest rate term. These are examples of product differences, not a recommendation or a promise that either fits your circumstances.

Check the minimum payment to you, how often it can arrive, and how long the schedule can continue. Also ask whether you can reduce, pause, or change it, and what process is required. A scheduled advance should not become a reason to borrow more each month than you need.

Remember that each payment adds borrowed money to the loan. If interest is not paid, the balance also grows with interest. A tidy monthly budget does not remove the cost or the effect on the equity left in your home.

Option 3: some money now and additional amounts later

Some products let you start with an initial advance and request other amounts when a need arises. That can be useful when the timing of an expense is uncertain.

Borrowing later can reduce the time that money accrues interest. But flexibility has its own rules. FCAC says an additional advance may bring a fee or a change to the interest rate. EQ Bank notes that single advances under a fixed interest rate term may be subject to a blended rate, meaning the rate may combine existing and new borrowing rather than simply preserving your original quote.

Confirm minimum amounts, fees, processing time, and which rate applies. Ask whether a later advance affects just the new money or the existing balance as well. Do not describe an unused amount as cash in your bank account or assume the arrangement works exactly like a HELOC.

Do not rely on an unwritten promise: HomeEquity Bank states that mortgage funding and subsequent advances are not guaranteed and remain subject to the loan agreement and underwriting guidelines. Before counting on future money for essential expenses, ask the chosen lender what could limit or stop it and which conditions must still be met.

Why a lower interest rate does not settle the question

A product intended for a single advance may have different pricing from one that offers more flexibility. EQ Bank currently describes Flex Lite as a product with a single advance at lower interest rates. That does not mean taking more cash earlier will necessarily cost less overall.

Compare the amount you actually need on the dates you need it. Taking a larger sum sooner means more money has longer to accrue interest. Waiting may reduce that time, but a higher rate, additional fees, or changed terms can offset the advantage.

Ask for side by side projections using the same spending plan and the same comparison dates. Include initial costs, later advance charges, interest, and the balance remaining if you expect to sell or move. If one option uses an adjustable rate, ask what happens if that rate rises. A projection illustrates assumptions; it cannot guarantee a future rate, balance, or home value.

A simple cash planning example

Imagine an Ontario homeowner who needs money for a specific repair and also expects a $600 monthly budget gap. It would be easy to add years of that gap to the repair cost and borrow everything immediately.

Instead, separate the confirmed repair amount from the recurring need. A $600 gap for five years adds up to $36,000 in cash advances, before interest, fees, and any initial advance. That arithmetic helps frame the question. It does not establish eligibility, a permitted advance amount, or the eventual loan balance.

Then ask how each available product would meet the same needs: cash now, a schedule of advances, or later requests. If a lender's minimum scheduled advance is more than $600, ask whether the product fits at all. Do not borrow extra simply because it is the minimum available.

Finally, plan for what happens after those five years. Would income change? Would expenses fall? Might you move? A loan can bridge a defined gap. It does not automatically solve a retirement budget that keeps getting harder to manage.

Questions to have answered before choosing

  • How much must I take initially, and how much will reach my bank account after debts and costs?
  • Does this exact product allow scheduled advances, occasional requests, or only a single advance?
  • What are the minimum amounts, timing, fees, and rate rules for later money?
  • Can a new advance change the rate on the balance I already owe?
  • How long can scheduled advances continue, and what makes them stop?
  • What conditions apply to later advances, and are further reviews required?
  • What happens if I need to pause the schedule, move, sell, or repay early?
  • What would I owe at the same future dates under each option, using clear assumptions?

Get the answers in writing and review the agreement with your independent lawyer. A product name and a rate are not enough to understand the borrowing plan.

Keep tomorrow's choices in the conversation

Whether you take one amount or a series of smaller advances, the home remains the security for the loan. You still need to budget for property taxes, insurance, upkeep, and the obligations in your agreement. Interest and fees can reduce the equity available for a later move or your estate.

Compare a reverse mortgage with other realistic choices, including using savings carefully, an affordable conventional loan or HELOC if you qualify, and downsizing. If you may leave the home soon, look at early repayment costs before committing.

I would rather start with the expense you are trying to solve than the largest number a calculator produces. The goal is a borrowing plan you understand, with enough room for the life you want to keep living.

Questions people ask about this

Can I receive monthly payments from a reverse mortgage in Ontario?

Some products offer scheduled advances, including monthly payments to you. Availability, minimum amounts, interest rate options, and the duration depend on the lender and product. These payments are borrowed money secured against your home, not pension income or a lifetime income guarantee.

Do I have to take the maximum amount I qualify for?

Not necessarily. Some products let you take an initial amount and request more later, subject to their terms. Others are designed for a single advance. Confirm the minimum initial amount, any existing debts to repay, and the written conditions for future borrowing.

Is taking smaller advances always cheaper?

No. Taking money later may reduce the time it accrues interest, but fees and a different interest rate can offset that benefit. Compare the same cash needs and time period under each option, using the actual product terms.

Are future reverse mortgage advances guaranteed?

Do not assume so. HomeEquity Bank states that funding and subsequent advances are subject to the loan agreement and underwriting guidelines and are not guaranteed. Ask each lender what could limit or stop later advances before relying on them for essential expenses.

Can a later advance change my interest rate?

It can. FCAC warns that an additional amount may bring a fee or a rate change. EQ Bank says single advances under fixed interest rate terms may be subject to a blended rate. Get the effect on your loan confirmed in writing before requesting more money.

How long will monthly advances last?

That depends on the available borrowing amount, the advance schedule, and the contract. Ask when the payments stop and how your budget will work afterward. A reverse mortgage should not be treated as a pension or an unlimited source of monthly money.

A reverse mortgage is a loan secured against your home. It must be repaid when you sell your home, move out permanently, or pass away. Interest accrues over time and reduces the equity remaining in your home. Terms, conditions, and lender qualification apply; approval, rates, and amounts are never guaranteed. A reverse mortgage is not a government program or benefit.
