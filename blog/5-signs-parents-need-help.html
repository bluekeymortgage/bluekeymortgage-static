5 Signs Your Parents Need Help at Home (And How to Start the Conversation)

By Ragini Domenichini, Mortgage Agent Level 2 · July 10, 2026 · 4-minute read

You visit your parents and something feels different. Sound familiar? You're not alone, thousands of Ontario families face this moment every year. Here's how to recognize the signs and start the conversation with love, not panic.

Sign 1: The house is slipping

A home that was always well-kept is suddenly cluttered, dusty, or showing signs of neglect. Mail is piling up. Dishes sit in the sink. The garden is overgrown.

What it usually means: daily tasks are becoming physically or mentally overwhelming. This isn't laziness, it's a signal that your parent may be struggling to keep up, often quietly, and often without wanting to admit it.

Sign 2: Changes in personal hygiene or appearance

Wearing the same clothes several days in a row. Unwashed hair. Skipping showers.

What it usually means: bathing and dressing take energy, balance, and motivation. A decline in personal care often points to physical difficulty, depression, or early cognitive changes, not carelessness.

Sign 3: Unexplained bruises, falls, or mobility issues

Your parent seems unsteady on their feet. They're holding onto furniture for balance. You notice bruises they can't quite explain.

What it usually means: falls are one of the most serious risks for seniors living alone. A single fall can lead to hospitalization and a permanent loss of independence, so this sign deserves immediate attention rather than a wait-and-see approach.

Sign 4: Weight loss or expired food in the fridge

The fridge is nearly empty, or full of expired items. Your parent seems thinner than last time you visited. Meals are being skipped or replaced with something quick from a box.

What it usually means: cooking requires planning, shopping, standing, and remembering, all of which can quietly become difficult. Unexplained weight loss can also point to depression or an underlying health issue worth checking with a doctor.

Sign 5: Social withdrawal or confusion

They're skipping activities they used to enjoy. Conversations seem more forgetful or confused than usual. Calls aren't being returned.

What it usually means: isolation and confusion can be early signs of cognitive decline or depression. Staying socially engaged matters for senior health, so a pullback from regular routines is worth paying attention to.

How to start the conversation

This is usually the hardest part. A few approaches that tend to work better than others:

  • Lead with love, not fear. Try "I've been thinking about you and want to make sure you're comfortable and safe" rather than "You can't take care of yourself anymore."
  • Ask, don't tell. "How are things going with the house?" or "Is anything feeling harder than it used to?" and let them open up at their own pace.
  • Frame it around independence. Position help as a way to stay in their home longer, not as a step toward leaving it.
  • Start small. A cleaning service, meal delivery, or a few hours of companionship a week is often an easier first step than jumping straight to full-time care.
  • Involve them in the decision. Offer options rather than ultimatums: "Would you rather have help with meals, or with cleaning?"

You don't have to figure this out alone

Many families don't realize that professional home care can start with just a few hours a week, and that it's often more affordable than they expect. Questions people ask about this

What if my parent refuses any help at all?

This is common, and pushing harder rarely works. Try smaller, less threatening offers first, a single visit from a cleaning service or a trial week of companionship care, framed as something to try rather than a permanent change. Many seniors come around once they see it doesn't threaten their independence.

Should I talk to my siblings before talking to my parents?

Usually, yes. A united message from adult children, even a brief conversation to agree on tone and next steps, tends to land better than one sibling raising it alone, and it avoids parents feeling like they're being managed by one child while others disagree.

When does this move from a home care conversation to a legal one?

If cognitive decline is significant enough to affect decision-making, it may be time to discuss power of attorney for property and personal care, if that isn't already in place. That's a separate step from arranging home care, and worth raising with an estate lawyer sooner rather than later.
