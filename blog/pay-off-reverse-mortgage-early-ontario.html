Can You Pay Off a Reverse Mortgage Early in Ontario?

By Ragini Domenichini, Mortgage Agent Level 2 · October 5, 2026 · 8 minute read

Yes. You can normally repay a reverse mortgage early, in part or in full. The important question is what your contract allows without a charge, because the rules and timing vary by lender and product.

The short answer

A reverse mortgage does not lock you into keeping the debt forever. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada says you usually have the option to repay principal and interest in full at any time. It also says your lender may allow voluntary payments up to a maximum amount.

That flexibility does not always mean the payment is free. An early repayment charge may apply, especially when you repay more than the contract permits or close the mortgage outside a charge free window.

Before moving money, ask the lender to confirm the exact privilege or payout in writing. A general website description is useful, but your signed contract controls.

“Paying it off early” can mean four different things

People use the same phrase for several different actions. The cost can change depending on which one you mean.

  1. Paying some or all of the accrued interest. This may slow or stop the balance from growing for a period of time.
  2. Making a partial payment against principal. This reduces the amount on which future interest is calculated.
  3. Repaying the entire balance while keeping the home. You may use savings, another mortgage, sale of an investment, or help from family.
  4. Paying the balance when the home is sold. The lawyer normally sends the required payout from the sale proceeds and arranges the discharge.

Tell the lender exactly what you plan to do. Asking only, “Can I make a payment?” may not produce the information you need about the amount, timing, and charge.

Why a prepayment charge may apply

Reverse mortgage lenders price a loan on the expectation that it will remain outstanding for a period of time. A contract may therefore set limits on how much principal you can repay, how often you can pay it, and when you can repay the full balance without a charge.

The charge can depend on:

  • The lender and reverse mortgage product
  • The version and date of your contract
  • Whether you are paying interest, principal, or the full balance
  • How long the mortgage has been open
  • The payment date or interest reset date
  • The reason the loan is being repaid

There may also be a statement fee, discharge fee, legal cost, or other closing cost. A prepayment charge is only one part of the final amount.

Current lender examples show why the contract matters

HomeEquity Bank publishes different repayment privileges for different contract versions. Its current borrower information says that whether a charge applies depends on the product, the date the mortgage was obtained, and the terms of the specific agreement. Some published contract versions permit scheduled interest payments and one annual partial payment, while older versions use different anniversary or reset windows.

EQ Bank currently says that, subject to conditions, a borrower may repay outstanding interest once per calendar month and make one principal prepayment of up to 10% in each 12 month period beginning on the initial advance date or its anniversary. It also publishes additional privileges after five and ten years. Its calculator is an estimate and does not include every exception or cost.

These examples are not promises about your mortgage. They show why advice from a neighbour, an old article, or even another customer of the same lender may not match your contract.

Do not send a lump sum based on a percentage you saw online. First ask the lender how the allowed amount is calculated, the dates when it can be paid, and how the money will be applied.

Could voluntary payments save money?

They can. Reverse mortgage interest is normally added to the balance. Reducing the balance sooner means less debt is left to earn interest in the future.

Paying some or all of the accrued interest may help keep the balance steadier. Paying principal reduces the base used for future interest calculations. Over many years, that can preserve more home equity for a future move, care costs, or an estate.

The benefit depends on the amount, timing, interest rate, and how long the mortgage remains open. Ask for an illustration using your actual balance rather than relying on a generic online example.

A simple payout example

Suppose a written payout statement shows:

  • $274,800 in principal and accrued interest
  • $4,200 as an early repayment charge
  • $1,000 in estimated legal, statement, and discharge costs
  • $280,000 as the approximate total needed

If you planned around the online balance of $274,800, you would be short by about $5,200. This is only an illustration. Actual figures and fees vary, and the payout changes as interest accrues. The lender and lawyer must provide the current numbers.

When paying early may make sense

A voluntary payment or full payout may deserve consideration when:

  • You have cash that is not needed for emergencies or near term living costs
  • You want to slow the growth of the balance and preserve more equity
  • You are selling the home or moving and must repay the loan
  • You qualify for a lower cost replacement and can comfortably manage its payments
  • A family member wants to help reduce the balance after everyone receives independent advice
  • Your contract offers a useful payment window that fits your plan

When it may be better to wait or pay less

Paying debt can feel automatically prudent, but using every available dollar can create a different problem. It may be better to keep more cash or make a smaller payment when:

  • The payment would leave too little for property taxes, insurance, repairs, care, or emergencies
  • A large prepayment charge applies and a lower cost window is close
  • You would need to sell an investment without understanding the tax and income consequences
  • You expect to need the money again and may not be able to borrow it back
  • The replacement loan has required payments that would strain the household budget

A reverse mortgage is designed to remove required regular principal and interest payments. Replacing it with a cheaper loan is not automatically better if the new payment is not comfortably affordable.

What happens if you sell the home?

When a home with a reverse mortgage is sold, the mortgage is normally repaid from the sale proceeds. The lawyer handling the closing obtains the lender's payout statement, sends the required money, and arranges to discharge the mortgage from title.

The amount left belongs to the homeowner after the mortgage, selling costs, legal costs, and any other secured debts are paid. My guide to selling a home with a reverse mortgage in Ontario explains the closing steps and remaining equity in more detail.

How to get an exact answer before you pay

  1. Find the contract and most recent statement. Note the product name, contract version if shown, initial advance date, and reset date.
  2. Ask about the payment privilege. Confirm the amount, frequency, timing, and whether it applies to interest, principal, or both.
  3. Request a written payout statement. Use the date when you expect to pay or close, because the balance can change each day.
  4. Ask for every charge separately. Include the prepayment charge, statement fee, discharge fee, legal cost, and any other amount.
  5. Confirm how the payment will be applied. Ask whether interest and fees are paid before principal and what balance will remain.
  6. Compare the alternatives. Look at the cost of paying now, waiting for another date, making a smaller permitted payment, or keeping the mortgage.
  7. Get advice before moving a large sum. A lawyer, tax professional, or financial advisor can help where legal, tax, investment, or estate issues are involved.

Questions to ask the lender

  • How much can I pay without a charge today?
  • When does my next payment privilege or reset window begin?
  • Does the limit apply to principal, interest, or the total amount owing?
  • How often can I make a payment?
  • What charge applies if I pay more than the permitted amount?
  • Are there different rules if I sell, move into long term care, or die?
  • What fees apply to a full payout and discharge?
  • How long is the written payout statement valid?

Questions people ask about this

Can I pay off a reverse mortgage early?

Yes. You can normally repay a reverse mortgage in full at any time, but an early repayment charge and other payout costs may apply. The exact amount depends on the lender, product, contract, and timing.

Can I make payments without paying off the whole reverse mortgage?

Often, yes. Many reverse mortgage contracts allow certain interest or principal payments without a charge, but the amount, frequency, and timing vary. Check the written terms before sending money.

Will I pay a penalty if I repay my reverse mortgage?

You may. A charge can apply when a payment exceeds the contract's privileges or when the loan is repaid early. Some contracts provide charge free payment windows or exceptions. Ask the lender for a dated written payout statement.

What happens when I sell a home with a reverse mortgage?

The reverse mortgage is normally paid and discharged from the sale proceeds through the lawyer handling the closing. A prepayment charge and legal or discharge costs may apply, depending on the contract and timing.

How do I get the exact reverse mortgage payout amount?

Ask the lender for a written payout statement for the expected payment or closing date. It should show the principal, accrued interest, any prepayment charge, and other applicable fees.

A reverse mortgage is a loan secured against your home. It must be repaid when you sell your home, move out permanently, or pass away. Interest accrues over time and reduces the equity remaining in your home. Terms, conditions, and lender qualification apply; approval, rates, and amounts are never guaranteed. A reverse mortgage is not a government program or benefit.
