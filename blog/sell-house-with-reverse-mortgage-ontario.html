Reverse mortgage basics

Can You Sell a House With a Reverse Mortgage in Ontario?

By Ragini Domenichini, Mortgage Agent Level 2 · September 24, 2026 · 8 minute read

Yes, you can sell. You still own the home. The important part is knowing what must be repaid at closing, what a prepayment charge could cost, and how much equity should remain for your next step.

The short answer

You can generally sell a house that has a reverse mortgage. When the sale closes, the reverse mortgage must be paid in full from the sale proceeds. You receive the equity that remains after the mortgage payout and the other costs of selling are paid.

The process is similar to selling a home with a regular mortgage. Your real estate lawyer obtains a payout statement, sends the required amount to the lender, and arranges for the mortgage to be discharged from title. The main difference is that a reverse mortgage balance has usually grown over time because interest is added instead of being paid each month.

You still own the home

A reverse mortgage is a loan secured against your home. It does not make the lender the owner. You decide whether to list the property, which real estate professional to use, and whether to accept an offer.

The lender is entitled to be repaid when a sale or transfer triggers the mortgage due date. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada lists selling the home as one of the events that requires repayment. Until closing, you still need to meet the mortgage obligations, including property taxes, home insurance, and reasonable maintenance.

What gets paid at closing?

The lender's payout statement may include more than the original amount borrowed. It can include:

  • The principal you received
  • Interest that has accrued up to the closing date
  • Any lender or discharge fees allowed by the contract
  • A prepayment charge, if one applies

The payout amount can change from day to day because interest continues to accrue. An estimate obtained before the home is listed is helpful for planning, but your lawyer will request the final figure for the actual closing date.

Ask for two numbers. Request an estimated total payout for your likely closing month and a separate explanation of any prepayment charge. That makes it easier to compare closing dates and understand what is interest, what is principal, and what is a charge for ending the mortgage.

How the sale works, step by step

  1. Contact the lender before listing. Ask what notice is required, how to request a payout statement, and whether the expected timing creates a prepayment charge.
  2. Choose an Ontario real estate lawyer. Give the lawyer the lender's information and a copy of the reverse mortgage documents.
  3. List and sell the home. The property can be marketed much like any other home. Make sure the closing date leaves enough time for the lawyer to obtain the final payout and discharge instructions.
  4. Your lawyer completes the payout. On closing, the buyer's funds are received in trust. The lawyer pays the reverse mortgage and other amounts that must be cleared from title.
  5. You receive the remaining proceeds. After the mortgage, legal costs, real estate costs, and other adjustments are paid, the remaining money belongs to you.

A simple example

Suppose an Ontario home sells for $900,000. The reverse mortgage payout on the closing date is $310,000. Assume the combined real estate, legal, moving, and other selling costs are $55,000.

In this simplified example, about $535,000 would remain:

  • $900,000 sale price
  • Minus $310,000 reverse mortgage payout
  • Minus $55,000 estimated selling and moving costs
  • Equals about $535,000 remaining

This is an illustration, not a quote. The actual amount depends on the sale price, the lender's payout, the contract, real estate commission, legal bill, property tax adjustments, moving expenses, and any other debts registered against the home.

Will there be a prepayment charge?

Possibly. Selling the home does not automatically mean every lender waives the charge. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada warns that paying a reverse mortgage before it is due may result in a fee and says the repayment terms come from your agreement.

HomeEquity Bank's current explanation of selling a home with a reverse mortgage also says a charge can apply when a mortgage is ended before the contract term is complete. EQ Bank's reverse mortgage FAQ explains that repaying the entire balance or exceeding a prepayment allowance may also create a charge, subject to its product conditions.

Ask for the charge in writing for the date you expect to close. A different date, rate reset, notice period, or reason for repayment may change the amount under some contracts. Do not choose a closing date based on a general website example alone.

What if the balance is close to the home's value?

Several Canadian reverse mortgage products include some form of no negative equity protection, but it is not wise to treat that phrase as an unlimited promise. Conditions matter.

EQ Bank currently says the amount owing on the due date will not exceed the home's fair market value if the mortgage obligations were met, while excluding certain fees and interest that may accrue after the due date. HomeEquity Bank describes its own guarantee on its selling guide. Your contract may define fair market value, deadlines, fees, and borrower obligations differently.

If the expected sale price is near or below the balance, involve the lender and lawyer before accepting the offer. This is especially important for a private sale, a sale to a relative, an unusual property, or an offer well below recent comparable sales.

Can you buy another home afterward?

Yes. The net sale proceeds are yours and can be used toward a smaller home, a condominium, rent, a retirement residence, or another goal.

The existing reverse mortgage does not automatically move to the next property. If you want a reverse mortgage on the new home, the property and borrowers will need to qualify under the lender's current rules. Coordinate the sale, purchase, and financing early so one closing is not relying on money that will arrive too late.

Seven questions to ask before you list

  1. What is the estimated mortgage payout for my likely closing month?
  2. Would a prepayment charge apply, and could the amount change with the closing date?
  3. How far in advance should my lawyer request the final payout statement?
  4. Does the lender require notice or any forms before the home is listed or sold?
  5. What fees will be added to the payout?
  6. What conditions apply to any no negative equity guarantee?
  7. If I am buying another home, must I complete a new application?

Special situations need a slightly different plan

If the sale follows a permanent move into care, repayment timing and possible charge reductions can be lender specific. Read what happens when you move into long term care and get the lender's answer in writing.

If the last borrower has died, the estate and executor have different responsibilities and deadlines. My article on what happens to a reverse mortgage when you die explains that process.

If a family member hopes to keep the property, speak with the lender and an independent lawyer before changing title or signing a private agreement. Keeping the home usually requires another source of funds to repay the reverse mortgage by the lender's deadline.

Questions people ask about this

Can I sell my house if I have a reverse mortgage?

Yes. You still own the home and can generally sell it. The reverse mortgage balance must be repaid when the sale closes, along with any interest, fees, and applicable prepayment charge. You keep the equity that remains after the loan and selling costs are paid.

Does the reverse mortgage lender take all the sale money?

No. Your lawyer uses the sale proceeds to pay the reverse mortgage and other amounts that must be cleared from title. The remaining net proceeds belong to you after legal, real estate, and other selling costs are paid.

Is there a penalty for selling a home with a reverse mortgage?

There may be a prepayment charge, depending on the lender, the mortgage contract, and when the sale closes. Ask the lender for the charge in writing for your expected closing date instead of assuming it will be waived.

What if the house sells for less than the reverse mortgage balance?

Some Canadian reverse mortgage lenders provide a no negative equity guarantee when the borrower has met the mortgage obligations, but the conditions and exclusions vary. Have your lawyer review the contract and confirm how the lender determines fair market value before accepting an unusual or below market offer.

Can I use the remaining equity to buy another home?

Yes, the remaining net sale proceeds are yours and may be used toward another home. The existing reverse mortgage does not automatically move to the next property, so any new financing requires its own lender review and approval.

Interest accrues over time and reduces the equity remaining in your home. Terms, conditions, and lender qualification apply; approval, rates, and amounts are never guaranteed.
