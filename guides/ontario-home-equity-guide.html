1. What a reverse mortgage actually is

A reverse mortgage is a loan secured against your home. It lets an eligible homeowner borrow from home equity while continuing to own the property.

  1. Regular loan payments are generally not required. Interest is added to the balance. Repayment is due on the events specified in your contract, including a sale, moving out, the last borrower's death or default.
  2. Eligibility depends on the lender. These loans are usually for homeowners 55 or older using their principal residence. Age, property condition, appraisal and lender criteria affect the amount.
  3. The borrowed funds are not taxable income. The loan itself does not reduce OAS or GIS. Ask a tax adviser about how you plan to use the funds.

Your home, and your responsibilities

You stay on title, with a mortgage registered against the home. Property taxes, insurance, maintenance, occupancy and all other contract obligations still matter. A breach can make the loan repayable; staying in the home alone is not a guarantee.

How much could you access?

There is no guaranteed amount based on age alone. Ask for a lender assessment showing the approved limit, existing debt to repay, fees and the cash left for your purpose.

How you receive the money

Depending on the product, you may receive a lump sum, regular advances or a combination. Minimum advances, additional fees and rate changes may apply. Compare the cost of taking funds now with drawing them later.

2. Compare Canadian lenders and contracts

Canadian reverse mortgages are different from US products such as HECM loans. Check the Canadian lender, its regulator and its written agreement rather than relying on US rules.

Products and providers change. Federally and provincially regulated institutions can have different consumer protections. Do not assume that every lender has identical terms.

3. The "living inheritance"

Here's a pattern I see constantly in Ontario: a couple in their seventies, mortgage-free home worth $800,000 or more, careful savers their whole lives, watching their forty-year-old kids battle a housing market and cost of living unlike anything they faced at that age.

The traditional plan is: live carefully, protect the house, leave it all behind someday. And for some families that's exactly right.

But more and more families are asking a different question: what if some of that help arrived while everyone's alive to enjoy it?

What a living inheritance looks like

  • A down payment for a child: turning years of their rent into equity of their own, when it changes their life the most.
  • A grandchild's education: paid without anyone taking on student debt.
  • Your own comfort, guilt-free: the home care, the renovation, the trip, the breathing room. Ask your family how they feel about supporting your comfort now and the effect on a later inheritance.

The honest tradeoff, in one sentence

Borrowing for a living inheritance means using some of your home's value now and may leave less for your estate later, because the loan balance grows with compounding interest until it's repaid. Section 5 shows you exactly what that looks like in numbers, because you should see the price of generosity before choosing it.

A thought worth sitting with: an inheritance at 60 renovates a kitchen. Help at 35 changes the trajectory of a life. Neither is wrong, but only one of them do you get to watch.

Two guardrails if you're considering this

  1. Your security comes first. I will never recommend gifting equity you may need for your own care later. We plan for that explicitly, it's Section 8 of this guide.
  2. Tell the whole family. Gifts that surprise siblings at the estate stage cause the fights you're trying to prevent. Openness now is cheaper than lawyers later.

4. The honest pros and cons

The potential advantages

  • Access part of your equity while retaining ownership.
  • Regular loan payments generally are not required, which may help monthly cash flow.
  • Borrowed money is not taxable income.
  • Qualification differs from a regular mortgage, although lender eligibility still applies.
  • Advance and voluntary-payment options may offer flexibility, depending on the contract.

The important drawbacks

  • Rates are usually higher than a regular mortgage or HELOC; compare actual offers.
  • Unpaid interest increases the loan balance and may reduce remaining equity.
  • Appraisal, setup, legal, closing and early repayment costs may apply.
  • A larger debt can leave less for future care, a move or the estate.
  • The loan must be repaid on contractual triggers. Other borrowing against the home may be restricted.
The plan matters. Compare what you need, how long you may stay, future care costs and alternatives. An option can fit one household and be unsuitable for another; no product is right for everyone.

5. What it costs, and how the balance grows

Get the full cost before deciding

Ask for written appraisal, lender setup, legal and closing fees, plus the early repayment schedule. Some fees may be added to the loan; others may be payable upfront. Do not assume there is nothing to pay out of pocket.

A simplified ten-year example

Assume a $300,000 loan and a $900,000 home, with a purely illustrative 6.5% annual interest rate compounded once a year, no payments, no further advances and no fees added. Hold the home's value constant to show the effect of interest alone.

Arithmetic illustration, not a current rate quote, offer or forecast. Actual contracts may use a different accrual/compounding method. Home values can rise or fall; fees, rate changes and later borrowing affect the outcome.

The balance grows even when your home value does not. Ask for a projection using the actual lender offer, including a flat or falling home-value scenario. Do not rely on appreciation to cover interest.

Optional payments: ask whether you can pay interest or part of the balance, what limits apply and whether a charge applies. Payments may limit debt growth; they do not guarantee an unchanged estate.

7. What happens at the end?

When the last borrower dies

The balance generally becomes repayable. Lenders set the deadline and consequences in their contracts; do not assume six months or automatic extensions. Your executor should obtain a written payout and legal advice promptly.

When a spouse survives or moves to care

The answer depends on who is a borrower, who remains in the home and the contract's occupancy terms. Ask the lender how a death, absence or move to care affects the loan. Do not assume an unchanged rate, no requalification or an automatic exemption.

When everyone moves out or the home is sold

A sale or permanent move can trigger repayment. Your lawyer can explain the payout and discharge. Family members may be able to keep the property by arranging another source of funds, subject to qualification and legal requirements.

What could remain for the family?

Remaining equity depends on the home's value, the payout and costs. Ask your lawyer whether a lender's equity protection applies and what its conditions exclude. Do not promise a particular inheritance or a universal no-shortfall outcome.

Plan together: give your estate lawyer and executor the lender's actual terms. A will's intentions, a mortgage payout deadline and an estate's practical ability to raise funds are different questions.

8. Planning for care in Ontario

Start with the services needed and current written quotes, rather than a general price range. Public eligibility and support, private providers, accommodation and optional services can affect the bill.

OptionBudget questions
Home and community careAsk a care coordinator what publicly funded services you qualify for, availability and any gaps to cover privately.
Private home careGet hourly/minimum-visit charges, planned hours, taxes, travel fees and backup-cover costs in writing.
Retirement residenceCompare accommodation, care, meals, extras, increases and the cancellation terms.
Long-term careCheck Ontario's current accommodation co-payments and basic-room support; confirm eligibility and costs with the home.

Work out the gap

Use your actual after-tax income, living costs and care quotes. Compare public support, savings and other resources before considering a loan. If home equity is an option, include the growing debt, setup costs and the possibility of a later move.

Renovating to stay: get an occupational therapist's assessment and contractor quotes. Check current CRA Home Accessibility Tax Credit rules for the relevant tax year; eligibility is not automatic and a credit is not an upfront grant.

9. Red flags to take seriously

Pause and check independently when someone pressures you or makes the process hard to verify.

  1. Pressure to sign. Offers and rate holds can expire. Ask for the deadline in writing; expiry is not a reason to skip independent advice.
  2. Unclear fees or unusual payment demands. Obtain a written cost breakdown, verify the payee independently and do not pay by gift card or cryptocurrency.
  3. Secrecy or isolation. Involve a trusted person if you want to. You should be free to ask questions and seek another view.
  4. Unverifiable identities. Check mortgage agents, brokers and brokerages on FSRA's registry, and independently verify the lender. Do not rely on a short list of brand names.
  5. Guaranteed approval or amounts. A lender must assess the application and property. Treat a promise before assessment with caution.
  6. US rules presented as Canadian protections. Confirm Canadian terms rather than assuming FHA or HECM rules apply.
  7. Unexpected title or authority documents. A mortgage registers a charge. Have your own lawyer explain any ownership transfer or power-of-attorney request before signing.
  8. Unrequested bundled services. Ask who is licensed for each service, how they are paid and what conflicts or optional costs exist.
A useful rule: make time for independent review. If you suspect fraud, contact your financial institution, FSRA or the police through a number you verify yourself.

  • "Are you licensed, and with whom?": Then verify the brokerage and agent on FSRA's public registry yourself. (Me: Ragini Domenichini, Mortgage Agent Level 2, BRX Mortgage Inc., FSRA #13463.)
  • "How are you paid, and by whom?": The answer should be immediate and specific. (Me: ask who pays the brokerage and confirm all borrower fees in writing.)
  • "Which lenders did you compare for me?": Ask which available lenders and products were considered, and why others were not.
  • "Show me the balance in 5 and 10 years, in writing.": The compounding projection. Refusing or hand-waving this is disqualifying.
  • "What are ALL the costs?": Appraisal, lender fee, legal, and any prepayment charges by year.
  • "What happens if I die next year? If my spouse does? If we move to care?": The answers should match Section 7 of this guide.
  • "When is this product wrong for someone like me?": The most revealing question on this list. Anyone who says "it's right for everyone" is selling, not advising.
  • "Can my daughter/son join our next call?": Watch the reaction closely. Welcome = good sign. Hesitance = leave.
  • "What alternatives should I rule out first?": You should hear HELOC, refinancing, and downsizing discussed honestly, with real tradeoffs.
  • "Can I take less than the maximum?": Ask about minimum advances and fees. Compare borrowing only what you need with taking the maximum.

11. The family conversation, a gentle script

Family misunderstandings can arise when a mortgage decision is shared late, framed as a done deal. Here's how to do it well, whichever side of the conversation you're on.

If you're the homeowner telling your kids

  • Lead with the why, not the product. "I want to stop worrying about money every month" or "I'd rather help you with a house now than leave you one later", the goal makes sense before the tool does.
  • Bring the numbers. Show the written projection (Section 5). Vagueness breeds worry; specifics settle it.
  • Name the inheritance thing out loud. "This means the estate will be smaller. I want to hear how you feel about that." You may be surprised, most adult children care far more about your comfort than the residual.
  • Invite them into the process, not just the announcement: the consultation call, the lawyer meeting. A shared discussion can expose assumptions and reduce misunderstandings.

If you're the adult child raising it with a parent

  • Ask, don't tell. "How are you feeling about money these days?" opens doors that "You should look at a reverse mortgage" slams shut. It's their home and their call.
  • Do the protective homework together: verify licences, read this guide side by side, join the calls. Being an ally beats being a gatekeeper.
  • Check your own conflict of interest, kindly. If the plan involves helping you, be the one who insists on the lawyer, the projection, and the pace. That protects your parent, and protects the plan from resentment later.
  • If siblings exist, loop them in early. The estate-stage surprise is the fight that breaks families. An early group conversation may help reduce that risk.
Standing offer: I do family calls all the time, parents at the kitchen table, kids dialed in from Calgary or London or down the street. Everyone hears the same numbers at the same time, and every question gets answered in front of everyone. It helps everyone understand the assumptions, obligations and tradeoffs together.
